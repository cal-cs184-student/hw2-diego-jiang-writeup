Section I: Bezier Curves and Surfaces

Part 1: Bezier curves with 1D de Casteljau subdivision

De Casteljau's algorithm evaluates a Bezier curve by repeatedly interpolating between adjacent control points at a fixed parameter \(t\), where \(0 \leq t \leq 1\). Each interpolated point is computed using \(p'_i = (1-t)p_i + tp_{i+1}\). Each level reduces the number of points by one, until the final point lies on the curve at that value of t.

I implemented evaluateStep by looping through adjacent pairs of input points and adding their interpolated positions to a new vector with push_back. The function uses the curve's member variable t, leaves the input unchanged, and returns only one evaluation level. For my six-control-point curve, five steps reduce the six original points to one. The screenshots show each level, the completed curve, and a modified curve after moving control points and changing t.

Part 2: Bezier surfaces with separable 1D de Casteljau

De Casteljau's algorithm extends to Bezier surfaces by applying the one-dimensional evaluation in two directions. First, each row of the control-point grid is evaluated at \(u\), producing one 3D point per row. These points are then evaluated at \(v\) to obtain the surface point at \((u,v)\).

I implemented evaluateStep using the same linear interpolation as Part 1, with Vector3D points and an explicit parameter t. evaluate1D repeatedly calls this function until one point remains. Finally, evaluate evaluates every row at u and applies evaluate1D to the resulting points at v. The screenshot shows bez/teapot.bez evaluated by this implementation.

Section II: Triangle Meshes and Half-Edge Data Structure

Part 3: Area-weighted vertex normals

I implemented Vertex::normal by traversing the triangles incident to a vertex using h->twin()->next(). For each non-boundary face, I compute the cross product \((p_1-p_0) \times (p_2-p_0)\). Its magnitude is twice the triangle's area, so summing these vectors automatically weights the face normals by area. I normalize the final sum to obtain a unit vertex normal and use HalfedgeCIter because the function is const.

The screenshots compare dae/teapot.dae before and after toggling vertex normals with Q. Flat shading makes individual triangles visible, while Phong shading with the area-weighted vertex normals produces smoother lighting across the surface.

Part 4: Edge flip

I implemented flipEdge by replacing the shared diagonal of two triangles with the edge connecting their opposite vertices. I first return immediately for boundary edges. For an interior edge, I store the six surrounding half-edges and their associated vertices, edges, and faces, then reassign their connectivity to form the two new triangles. I also update the representative half-edge pointers of the affected vertices, edges, and faces. The operation takes constant time and does not create or delete any mesh elements or move any vertices.

Keeping the half-edges grouped into two explicit face loops helped organize the pointer assignments. After several flips on the teapot, the changed diagonals produced longer, thinner triangles and noticeable shading differences.

Part 5: Edge split

I implemented splitEdge by inserting a vertex at the midpoint of the selected edge and connecting it to the opposite vertices of the two adjacent triangles. This replaces two triangles with four and adds one vertex, three edges, two faces, and six half-edges. I reassign the half-edge connectivity and update the representative pointers of the affected elements. The new vertex's half-edge points along a segment of the original edge. Each split performs a constant amount of work.

Grouping the pointer assignments by the four resulting triangle loops helped keep the connectivity organized. I also mark the two segments of the original edge as old and the connections to opposite vertices as new, preparing for Loop subdivision. Part 6: Loop subdivision for mesh upsampling

I implemented Loop subdivision in three stages: compute updated positions from the original mesh, subdivide the connectivity, and apply the stored positions. For an interior vertex of degree n, the updated position is \((1-nu)p+u\sum_i p_i\), where \(u=3/16\) for \(n=3\) and \(u=3/(8n)\) otherwise. For an interior edge with endpoints A and B and opposite vertices C and D, the inserted vertex uses \(3(A+B)/8+(C+D)/8\).

I store these values in newPosition without changing the original positions. I save the original edge iterators in a vector and split only those edges, avoiding repeated splits of newly created edges. I then flip new edges connecting one old and one new vertex, excluding the two segments of each original edge. Finally, I copy the stored positions to the vertices. The isNew flags distinguish the edges that need flipping, and computing all weights first prevents traversal order from affecting the result.

Subdivision Behavior and Sharp Features

Subdivision increases the triangle density and smooths the surface, as shown on the teapot. On the cube, sharp corners and edges become rounded because the updated positions average neighboring vertices.

To test whether pre-splitting reduces corner rounding, I pre-split the three sharp edges meeting at one cube corner before subdividing. This adds closer neighbors along those edges, but the corner still rounds substantially. The remaining face diagonals connect it to distant vertices, so this limited preprocessing does not preserve a sharp corner. Pre-splitting can reduce smoothing when it brings the whole local neighborhood closer to the feature; it does not impose a fixed crease.

Cube Asymmetry and Symmetric Preprocessing

Repeated subdivision of the original cube produces an asymmetric rounded shape. The diagonals used to triangulate its square faces give corners different neighbor arrangements and degrees, so the subdivision weights do not treat geometrically equivalent corners identically. These connectivity differences persist through later iterations.

To remove this bias, I split the diagonal on each of the six square faces. Each face gains a center vertex connected to its four corners, creating the same symmetric four-triangle pattern on every face. All cube corners then have equivalent neighborhoods, producing a symmetric rounded result. Flipping a diagonal alone changes which corners receive the extra connection but retains a preferred direction within the face. For this preprocessing, I used diagonal splits; additional flips were unnecessary. The subdivision routine itself still uses flips to form the four-way triangle subdivision.
